Real-rooted random polynomials · project explainer

Cusp and Cube

“What is the chance that a random cubic has all real roots?” is secretly a geometry question: it asks for the volume of a shape carved out of coefficient space by the discriminant. This page shows the shape — and the two closed forms our first session of machinery pulled out of it.

1 · Where this came from

From nine dimensions down to three

The original question was about matrices: pick a 3×3 matrix with nine independent uniform entries — what is the probability that all three eigenvalues are real? That is the volume of a blob cut out of a 9-dimensional cube by a degree-6 discriminant surface. The Gaussian version has a beautiful answer (2−n(n−1)/4, Edelman) because Gaussian measure has orthogonal symmetry; the uniform cube has none, and the problem stalls at simulation (P ≈ 0.7076, our own 107-sample run).

The fix is not to try harder in nine dimensions — it is to move the question to where it becomes geometric: coefficient space. A monic cubic x³ + ax² + bx + c is a point (a, b, c) in a 3-dimensional cube, and “all roots real” is a region in that cube. Same mathematical species as the matrix problem — a discriminant volume — but small enough to see, integrate, and prove things about.

9D: matrix entries 3×3 uniform matrix; no symmetry to exploit; simulation only, ~0.7076
→
3D: cubic coefficients (a, b, c) uniform in a cube; the real-rooted set is a concrete region
→
Volume = probability P(3 real roots) = vol{ Δ ≥ 0 } / vol(cube)
2 · The shape in two dimensions

The discriminant divides the plane

Reference · the discriminant, in general

Every polynomial has a discriminant — one number built from its coefficients that is zero exactly when the polynomial has a repeated root. Away from zero, its sign sorts how many of the roots are real.

quadraticax²+bx+c Δ2 = b² − 4ac
cubicax³+bx²+cx+d Δ3 = b²c² − 4ac³ − 4b³d − 27a²d² + 18abcd
Δ3 > 0 three distinct real roots
Δ3 = 0 a repeated root (all roots real)
Δ3 < 0 one real root, two complex-conjugate roots

Worked example: the monic quadratic. Even one level down — before cubics at all — the same moves already work. Drag the point.

b right, c up · drag anywhere
 

Δ2 ≥ 0 exactly when c ≤ b²/4 — the teal parabola. For fixed b the favorable c runs from the floor −1 up to b²/4 (the parabola never reaches the ceiling c=1, so nothing needs clipping), a length of b²/4 + 1; integrating over b ∈ [−1,1] gives area 13/6, and dividing by the square’s area 4: P(real roots) = 13/24 ≈ 0.5417.

Set a=1, b=0, c=p, d=q — the depressed cubic below — and Δ3 collapses to exactly the −4p³−27q² used in Figure 1. Every “3 real roots” region on this page, from the cusp to Theorem 1’s cube to Figure 3’s clipped tongue, is this same quantity staying nonnegative.

Start with the depressed cubic x³ + px + q (no square term), so coefficient space is just the (p, q) plane. The discriminant Δ = −4p³ − 27q² is positive inside a cusp-shaped region and negative outside. Drag the point and watch the cubic’s graph: inside the cusp it crosses the axis three times; outside, only once.

The teal region is bounded by the cusp curve 4p³ + 27q² = 0. Its area inside the square, divided by the square’s area 4, is the probability that a random depressed cubic with (p, q) uniform on [−1, 1]² has three real roots: exactly 2√3/45 = 0.076980… This exact value is the calibration anchor for all of our machinery (our 2×108-sample Monte Carlo lands within 0.1σ of it).
3 · The shape in three dimensions

Slicing the cube

Now the full monic cubic: (a, b, c) uniform in [−1, 1]³. Fix a and you get a 2D slice of the cube; the real-rooted region in each slice is a curved tongue in the (b, c) square. The whole probability is the average tongue area over all slices. Move the slider.

The key reduction. For fixed (a, b) with b < a²/3, the values of c giving three real roots form an interval [clo, chi] of width (4/27)(a² − 3b)3/2 — because the discriminant factors as Δ = −27 (c − clo)(c − chi) (verified symbolically). So the 3D volume collapses to a 2D integral of explicit band widths. And one geometric miracle makes it exactly solvable: the band never leaves the cube. It touches the ceiling c = 1 at exactly one corner point, (a, b, c) = (−1, −1, 1) — set a = −1 above and look at the top-left corner of the slice — so the messy “clipping corrections” everyone would expect are identically zero. Rotate the 3D view toward that corner and you can watch the solid’s tip touch it exactly, with no gap and no overflow.
4 · What the machinery found

Two closed forms, on day one

The research memo listed this as an open problem with “no published closed form.” The pipeline — exact reduction, high-precision quadrature, constant recognition, then a full symbolic proof — closed it in the first session. Every step below is verified three independent ways.

Theorem 1 · symmetric cube
For (a, b, c) i.i.d. uniform on [−1, 1]:
P( x³ + ax² + bx + c has 3 real roots )  =  383/4860 + ln(3)/48
= 0.10169434037605886959954086…
Theorem 2 · unit cube
For (a, b, c) i.i.d. uniform on [0, 1]:
P( x³ + ax² + bx + c has 3 real roots )  =  1/2880
= 0.000347222… — a plain rational: every algebraic term in the integral cancels.

Why so clean? Theorem 1’s integral is the “no clipping” integral plus the identity asinh(1/√3) = ln(3)/2. Theorem 2 splits at the curve b = a²/4, where the lower band edge clo changes sign; on each side the fractional powers cancel and the slice integral is the polynomial a⁵/480.

The method that worked here — reduce → integrate precisely → recognize the constant → prove symbolically — is the template for the quartic: its band structure in the leading slice coordinates is richer, but the same never-clipped question is the first thing to ask.

7 · Resolved: Theorem 3

Dropping the monic restriction

Theorems 1 and 2 both fixed the leading coefficient at 1. The more natural question drops that restriction: for ax³ + bx² + cx + d, all four coefficients i.i.d. uniform on [−1, 1] — including a itself, which can land anywhere near zero — what is P(3 real roots)?

Dividing through by a turns this into the same monic cubic already solved, just evaluated on a bigger box: (b/a, c/a, d/a) is uniform on [−1/a, 1/a]³. Writing V(t) for the volume of the real-rooted region inside the cube [−t, t]³ (so V(1) is exactly 8×Theorem 1), the whole problem becomes one integral:

P(non-monic, 3 real roots)  =  ∫01 (a³/8) · V(1/a) da

That needs V(t) for every t ≥ 1, not just t = 1 — and the never-clipped miracle behind Theorem 1 was an exact coincidence at t = 1 (the band touched the corner precisely there, no more, no less). Past that window the band spills over the edge and the clean closed form breaks. Drag the slider to see why — then read on for how it got solved anyway.

Below t = 1 the tongue sits fully inside the frame. Right at t = 1 its corner just kisses the frame’s edge — the exact configuration Theorem 1 exploits. Push past t = 1 and that corner is visibly sheared off: the missing sliver is the “clipping correction” that made Theorem 1 easy to avoid and makes this integral hard. (This on-page curve is a coarse illustrative estimate, not a precision result — the exact value is below.)

V(t) itself turns out not to have a nice closed form for t ≠ 1 — a blind search for one (PSLQ, against every constant that shows up elsewhere on this page) comes back empty. So integrating this way is a dead end. The route that actually worked is structural, not calculus: the discriminant is homogeneous — scale a, b, c, d by the same λ and Δ scales by λ⁴ — so the real-rooted region is a cone. The divergence theorem turns the volume of a cone intersected with a cube into a sum over the cube’s 8 faces, and symmetry collapses those 8 faces to exactly Theorem 1’s volume plus one new, purely 3-dimensional piece — which has an elementary closed form once the leading coefficient is integrated out first.

Theorem 3 · non-monic cube · resolved 2026-08-17
For (a, b, c, d) i.i.d. uniform on [−1, 1]:
P( ax³ + bx² + cx + d has 3 real roots )  =  641/2430 − ln(3)/24
= 0.21801049620261477108898412335868…

This is exactly the value the dxdy.ru forum thread had guessed back in June 2026 — but nobody, including that thread, had proved it. Verified five independent ways: sympy confirms the closed form exactly; a second numerical route sharing no step with the derivation agrees to 19 significant digits; a blind PSLQ search recovers the same closed form straight from 22-digit numerics; and 4×1010 Monte Carlo samples land within 0.8σ. A conflicting value this project had itself produced — a literature-sweep quadrature of 0.217993225 ± 5e-8 — is refuted: wrong by 1.7×10−5, about 345× its own claimed error bar. A genuine attempt to pin down exactly what went wrong in that computation came up empty, and that is recorded honestly rather than guessed at — though two real hazards in this family of integrals turned up along the way: a √a endpoint singularity as the leading coefficient vanishes, and float64 silently losing track of the clipping boundary nearby.
